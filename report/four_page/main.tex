\title{Answer agreement measures difficulty, not fragility: a robustness predictor and its self-audit}

\author{\textbf{Jude Wallis (Independent researcher)}}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
The AIMO Interpretability Challenge asks an entry to predict, from a math problem's original text, whether a model's
accuracy survives the organisers' perturbations. We ask what a working robustness predictor actually reads. Ours
mostly reads how often a stronger proxy model's repeated answers agree: on 63 labelled public cases, agreement
alone ranks robust above non-robust cases within each configuration with AUC 0.914, mostly because 30 of 31
cases on easy problems are robust; on hard problems it is 0.821, and on the same attempts it beats the proxy's answer-key
accuracy only within noise: it measures difficulty. Its errors look problem-level: of 16 problems labelled for several
configurations, 15 get one label from every configuration, against about 9 when labels are shuffled among problems
of similar agreement, on 80 cases; harsher perturbation of some problems would do the same. One miss
illustrates how: a model's skipped check changes nothing at the original numbers, so its answer is right there but
wrong once our rewrites change them. The entry, refitted without each problem, is right on 55 of
63 public cases, and on 15 of 21 main and 9 of 14 small cases of the hidden validation set, against 49, 10
and 7 for always answering robust; exact intervals exclude always robust only on the main board, barely, and the
small track missed our 12.2 prediction. Our self-audit finds that rules kept because the validation score rose
add no demonstrable accuracy, and that the score's rerun standard deviation is about 1.3 cases.
\end{abstract}

\section{The question and our answer}
\label{sec:intro}

The AIMO Interpretability Challenge \citep{stefanik2026} labels each pairing of a math problem with a model
configuration as robust or non-robust, by how far its accuracy falls on the organisers' perturbed versions. Seven configurations are judged: Qwen3.5-4B, Skywork-OR1-Math-7B, Olmo-3-7B-Think and DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B
(the small track) and, on the main track, also gpt-oss-120b at low, medium and high effort. An entry sees only the
original problem, runs each case alone, and in the final meets 30 to 50 new problems at about 170\,s per
case. Perturbed problems cut accuracy \citep{mirzadeh2025,huang2025a,mondorf2026}; whether interpretations predict
unseen behaviour is asked by \citet{belinkov2022}, \citet{li2025} and \citet{huang2025b}; agreement among sampled
answers is a known signal of correctness and difficulty \citep{wang2023,kuhn2023,farquhar2024}, as is a model's estimate
of what it knows \citep{kadavath2022}. Our interpretability question is what a working robustness predictor reads. \textbf{Our answer: how hard the problem is (Section~\ref{sec:label}); what it misses looks like
\emph{fragility}, the problem-level part of the label that agreement does not explain (Section~\ref{sec:miss}), which
no reading we tried caught (Section~\ref{sec:neg}).} New here are this split and the audit of our own entry (Section~\ref{sec:audit}).

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:data}

\textbf{Data.} We ran one text per template; 8 of the 71 labelled public cases were labelled on different numbers, so we
drop them and keep 63. All our public-case numbers use these 63 (49 robust) unless noted; Appendix~\ref{app:supp} gives every value on all labelled cases. 22 of the 63, on 12 AIME-2026 templates, are
\emph{newer problems}; they are not held out: we saw them while choosing hand-set values.

\textbf{The entry} reads only behaviour on the original. For Qwen, DeepSeek and gpt-oss low and medium cases, the proxy model (Table~\ref{tab:terms}) makes K attempts (K = 4 to 32, by time left), and a small logistic rule fitted on the labelled public cases
(Supplement F.4) calls the case from their agreement, whether any of 4 non-thinking Qwen3.5-4B attempts ends within
2,000 tokens, and whether the case is gpt-oss medium. For Skywork and Olmo cases it runs the judged model itself
(\emph{own runs}): robust iff at least 1 (Skywork) or 2 (Olmo) of 3 attempts end on their own inside a 9,000- or
10,000-token cap. gpt-oss high cases are always called robust (Section~\ref{sec:audit} says why). Time limits: Appendix~\ref{app:supp}.

\section{What agreement reads: difficulty}
\label{sec:label}

\textbf{The test.} If agreement captured more than difficulty, it should clearly beat a pure difficulty score, the proxy's
answer-key accuracy. We score a ranking by its \emph{within-configuration AUC}, the share of
same-configuration (robust, non-robust) case pairs it orders correctly; 95\% intervals resample templates. A
\emph{read} is one server run of K attempts on every public problem. On the 17 hard templates (39 pairs), over the same 80 attempts from six reads (K 8 or 16), agreement beats accuracy only
marginally, within noise: 0.897 against 0.872 (difference 0.025, interval 0.000 to 0.176); swapping accuracy into the
entry, refitted leaving each template out, changes 1 of 32 hard-case calls, from wrong to right. Agreement measures
difficulty. The judged model's solve rate predicts little: in a simulation of the organisers' labelling
fitted to public accuracy drops, a case is non-robust with probability 0.18 to 0.21 whether it solved the original
half the time or always (model-based).

\textbf{The headline numbers} (post hoc). One read at K = 32, by an earlier build of the entry (which uses 4 to 32
attempts as time allows), gives agreement AUC 0.914 (0.778 to 0.993) on all 105 pairs: our headline, a
score of the agreement feature, not of the entry (Section~\ref{sec:audit}). Most of it comes from the 10 easy
templates, where 30 of 31 cases are robust. On hard templates this read gives 0.821 (0.448 to 1.000), with 7 of 39
pairs misordered, tying the lowest of the six smaller reads (0.821 to
0.936). Agreement looks saturated on hard problems; all rest on 39 pairs.

\textbf{What agreement cannot see: T10}, a geometry problem summing the possible lengths of a triangle's side,
illustrates how a slip can hide at the given numbers. gpt-oss low agrees with itself more on T10 (0.762) than on the
robust T09 (0.655), yet T10 is non-robust for it. On two harsher rewrites of ours, its wrong answers are exactly those of
the correct method minus one check, a lower triangle bound. At the original numbers the check
removes nothing (both give 850): the condition is \emph{dormant}, and the slip is an \emph{equivalent mutant}, a changed
program with the same output on the tested input \citep{papadakis2015}. No answer key, agreement or accuracy at the
given numbers can show it; only numbers on which the condition binds can. The slip was seen in our harsher rewrites only
(gpt-oss medium makes it yet is robust), not shown to cause the organisers' label.

\section{What agreement misses: the problem's own fragility}
\label{sec:miss}

Grouped by exact problem text, the labels follow the problem, not the model (computed, post hoc): of the 16 problems
labelled for several configurations, 15 get one label from every configuration, and 4 of the 5 with a non-robust
label are non-robust for all; T10 is the exception. Reading no output of ours, this count uses all 80 labelled
cases, 9 labelled after our design (Appendix~\ref{app:supp}). \textbf{Difficulty does not explain it.} In a test we pre-registered, that is, wrote in
our dated records before the result existed (no external timestamp), shuffling each configuration's labels 10,000 times
among problems in the same third of agreement gives one label on 9.43 of the 16 on average, never 15 ($p \leq
0.0001$), and never 4 of 5 all non-robust; on the 71 cases labelled first, 35 shuffles reach 14 of 15 ($p = 0.0035$).
By our pre-set rule, the claim stands beyond difficulty. A second pre-registered check, adding the other
configurations' labels on the same problem to agreement in a logistic rule (leave-one-template-out), points the
same way but is not significant: AUC rises from 0.882 to 0.976, gain 0.094 (interval $-0.037$ to 0.286).
Caveats: few problems; difficulty finer than a third of agreement, or unseen by it, counts here as fragility; and if
some problems were perturbed harder, labels would follow the problem too.

\section{How well the entry generalises}
\label{sec:audit}
\label{sec:noise}

\textbf{Against always answering robust} (Table~\ref{tab:audit}). On public cases, each called by a rule refitted
without its template (leave-one-template-out), the entry is right on 55 of 63 (always robust: 49), and on 17 of the 22
newer problems (12). On the main board it scored 15 of 21 (always robust: 10), inside
the predicted range; earlier builds of the same rules scored 16 and 17. \textbf{On the small track the prediction
missed}: 9 or 10 of 14 against 12.2 predicted (always robust: 7). Exact 95\% intervals exclude always robust only on
the main board, barely (0.478 against 0.476): the small track's 9 of 14 is not distinguishable from always robust's 7
of 14 on its own, nor 55 of 63 (0.765 to 0.944) from 49.

\textbf{What was fitted, and when.} The logistic rule, the only part fitted to labels, was fitted on 5 October, before
the board-tested changes of 5 to 11 October (Appendix~\ref{app:supp}). About ten hand-set values escape
cross-validation. Uploads tested variants motivated by public cases, never per-case probes, though early ones tested
guesses about the hidden sets (Supplement E.6.2). Three rules tried there decide 8 of 21 main and 6 of 14 small cases.
The gpt-oss high constant was chosen from all public labels (robust 13 times in 15; the logistic rule did worse on
its hard cases); the board did not rise with it. The two own-run rules were kept because the
board rose (+3 in all).

\textbf{Did board feedback help?} If every changed answer were a coin flip, keeping a change only when the board rises
would still gain +3.9 cases in expectation (90\% range 1 to 8); if each were right 70\% of the time, +7.3 (3 to 12)
(model-based). The kept +3 lies inside both ranges, so this \emph{selection null} \citep{dwork2015,blum2015,roelofs2019}
cannot tell them apart: \textbf{the rules kept because the board rose add no demonstrable accuracy.} We keep them
because dropping them is not better supported: on 9 Skywork cases labelled after the choice, the own run beats the
proxy (8.0 against 7.25 right, averaged over reads), and a pre-registered switch back did not pass.

\textbf{Calls are not reproducible across days} (computed). With fixed seeds, same-day reruns match
exactly (23 of 23 checks), but in another \emph{server state} (another day), 26\% of agreement-based calls
flip (90\% interval 0.14 to 0.45); own-run and always-robust calls never did. \textbf{This is a reproducibility limit of
the entry, and its cause is unknown} (Appendix~\ref{app:supp}). One board total has a standard deviation of about 1.3 cases on main and 1.0 on
small, so a 21-case board cannot order entries whose readings resample and differ by two cases or fewer. Frozen forecast for the final: Appendix~\ref{app:supp}.

\section{What did not work}
\label{sec:neg}

Of 88 negative results (Supplement D), \textbf{70 failed a pre-registered bar}; 2 were unclear, 13 had no bar or a post hoc one, and 3 were not built or run. Table~\ref{tab:neg} shows
four. A probe of the judged model's internal state reads difficulty, and Qwen's solve rate in its place
loses cases, so \textbf{we make no White-Box (internal-signal) claim}. No fragility reading passed all its bars.
Programs that a Claude agent wrote after reading the
raters' notes scored 12 of 12 Skywork and Olmo cases of Table~\ref{tab:neg}'s set, blind ones 9 to 11: \textbf{a reading
computed through a solver's own steps needs a blind replication.}

\section{Lessons}
\label{sec:lessons}

Check labelled texts against the texts you read. Split a predictor's score by where labels vary, and test problem-level
claims with a difficulty-keeping null. Refit label-fitted parts leaving one template out, and compare validation
gains with a selection null. For benchmark builders: only numbers on which a
dormant condition binds can tell a method from its equivalent mutant.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

Reading noise rests on 3 server states; the newer problems are not held out; the own-run rules rest on thin, post hoc
support (3 Skywork and 9 Olmo cases when chosen); the raters, whose rubric a Claude agent wrote after seeing the
labels, and the blind writers are Claude subagents; pre-registration has no outside timestamp; the final is rehearsed
on a mock, not run.

\textbf{The forecast} (Section~\ref{sec:audit}). We expect v111 (v115's parent: same rules, looser limits) to get
\textbf{about 16 of 21 main cases right on new problems} (90\% range 11 to 20), not the 19 of our best board run, v65 (Codabench 976461; other draws of its recipe scored 16 to 18). Guess
ranges for final accuracy, frozen on 17 October, are 0.72 to 0.82 for v111 and 0.62 to 0.76 for v112.
On 30 to 50 new problems, reading noise (Section~\ref{sec:noise}) and problem sampling give an accuracy standard
deviation of about 0.07 to 0.09 per run; that noise alone would put the final inside them only about 40 to 50\% (v111) and 55 to 70\%
(v112) of the time (computed), so a hit is weak support; a miss by more than about 0.08 would count against us. The
assumptions, the hash and the rule that applies the ranges to the evaluated v115 and v116 follow.
